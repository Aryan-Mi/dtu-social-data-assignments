Part 2 · Where is the problem

It's everywhere — some places more than others

The gap is national, but its intensity varies by region. The Midwest shows the largest disparity, at 2 extra minutes per visit on average.

What an extra minute means
1 minute — time for one full set of cardiac compressions in CPR
2 minutes — average interval between contractions in active labour
3 minutes — window for clot-busting drugs to begin working in stroke care
5 minutes — brain cells begin dying without oxygen
Part 3 · The problem over time

It's getting better. Slowly.

The median wait gap between women and men has narrowed over the past decade — but the gap has not closed. As of the most recent data, women still wait longer at every triage level.

Women's median ER wait time has been consistently higher than men's from 2009 to 2022, though the gap has narrowed.

The gap shrank most meaningfully between 2014–2018, coinciding with increased national attention to triage equity. Progress since 2019 has stalled. Data: NHAMCS 2009–2022.

What we learned

The ER is not neutral

Across pain levels, triage categories, regions, and years — the pattern holds. When a woman and a man walk into an emergency room with the same symptoms, she will likely wait longer.

⏱

It's systematic, not random

The gap appears at every combination of pain level and triage acuity in the NHAMCS dataset. This rules out coincidence.

📍

Geography amplifies it

The Midwest gap of +2 min is twice the national average. Local protocols and hospital structures matter.

📈

It's improving — but not gone

The trend is encouraging. But improvement has stalled since 2019, and the gap remains present at every triage level.

💓

Minutes are not trivial

In cardiac arrest, stroke, and labor complications, the difference between 1 and 3 minutes is clinically significant.
