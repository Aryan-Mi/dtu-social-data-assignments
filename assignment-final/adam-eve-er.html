ACT V Outcome 02:30 — FOUR HOURS LATER
PATIENT A · ADAM

Admitted. Stent placed. Recovering.

Adam's troponin levels come back elevated. The cardiology team confirms an NSTEMI — a non-ST elevation heart attack. He is admitted to the cardiac care unit. A stent is placed the following morning. He is discharged five days later with a comprehensive care plan.

Adam's journey reflects the documented pattern in NHAMCS data: male patients presenting with chest pain are significantly more likely to receive cardiac diagnostic workups, cardiac-specific diagnoses, and cardiology referrals.

ACT V
PATIENT B · EVE

Discharged. Returns by ambulance three days later.

Eve takes her lorazepam and goes home. Three days later, she returns to the same ER by ambulance, unresponsive. She had suffered a massive MI. Though she ultimately survives, she sustains significant cardiac damage that would have been preventable with timely intervention.

Delayed

Eve's experience is consistent with published findings from NHAMCS analysis: women presenting with chest pain are more likely to receive a psychiatric or psychosomatic diagnosis on their first visit, delaying cardiac identification.

This is not a single story. In NHAMCS data covering over 30 years and hundreds of thousands of emergency visits, the patterns Adam and Eve represent appear consistently — in chest pain, abdominal pain, and across pain scores. The disparity is not a flaw in any one nurse or physician. It is structural, measurable, and ongoing.
