SPECIAL REPORT — ER WAIT TIMES

The Wait Gap.

Across 14 years, 323,137 emergency-room visits, and four U.S. census regions, sex remained an independent predictor of how long a patient waited to be seen. The pattern does not disappear when pain, triage, race, or year are held constant.

A field report from the NHAMCS public-use files CDC · National Center for Health Statistics Edition №14 / 2009–2022

The waiting room of an American emergency department is one of the few places in modern medicine where every patient theoretically becomes a number — a triage acuity, a chief complaint, a pain score on a ten-point scale. The system is designed to be sex-blind, race-blind, ZIP-code-blind.

It is not.

"Same pain. Same triage. Same year. Different wait. Every year."

The National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey is the federal government's longest-running probability sample of how Americans use hospital emergency care. Between 2009 and 2022 it sampled 323,137 distinct visits. In every one of those fourteen years, the median time a woman waited to see a provider was at least as long as a man's — and usually longer.

What follows is the evidence. The exhibits are in chronological order, but the conclusion is in every one of them.

— THE FINDING —

The ER is not neutral.

It is not chaos. It is not coincidence. It is not a single doctor on a single day. It is a fourteen-year, four-region, twenty-two-of-twenty-five-cells, race-compounded pattern — measured, weighted, and published by the federal government's own statistical agency. It is, by every definition this dataset can support, systemic.

NHAMCS · 2009 → 2022 · Submitted in evidence

About the data

The National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey (NHAMCS) is a probability sample of U.S. emergency departments operated by the National Center for Health Statistics, part of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Public-use files cover 1992–2022; the ED component is in continuous operation since launch.

About the measure

"Wait time" in this report refers to the recorded interval between a patient's arrival at an ED and the time the patient was first seen by a provider (MD, APRN, or PA). It is reported in NHAMCS as a continuous variable in minutes, and is what the agency uses in its own annual summaries.

About the limits

The public-use files report only four Census regions, so state-level claims in this issue are visual extensions of regional data. The clinical reference points in the Ruling section (CPR, thrombolysis, labour) are illustrative of what a one- to three-minute window means biologically, not direct outcome measurements from this dataset.
